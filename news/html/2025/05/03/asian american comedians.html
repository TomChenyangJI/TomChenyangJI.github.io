Four Asian American Comics on the Joke That Changed Their Life

Bowen Yang, Hari Kondabolu, Atsuko Okatsuka and Hasan Minhaj break down the stand-up bits that rewired their comedic DNA.

Bowen Yang, Hari Kondabolu, Atsuko Okatsuka and Hasan Minhaj break down the stand-up bits that rewired their comedic DNA.

Bowen Yang on Sabrina Wu

‘You can be angry and you can be funny about it’

YANG I met Sabrina for the first time, I think in like 2018. My friend Matt Rogers and I were hosting this show where we had 50 comedians on. And I think it was my manager who passed Sabrina along to us. And because I hadn’t met them before, I wasn’t familiar with their work. But they were like, You should book this comedian Sabrina Wu. They’re very funny.

The bit is basically Sabrina on stage being like, I was forced to read this book growing up.

I think they give the author some grace. They’re not like, This sucks. It’s so hard to be Asian. They’re just like, It’s wild that I grew up reading a book about a Chinese boy when everything about it was fabricated and in the imagination of some kooky white lady.

Sabrina gives you just enough context for what the bit is, and then they’re like, None of that is Chinese. And then they’re like, Let’s play a game!

It’s working on both ends of the same axis: You can be angry and you can be funny about it. And I think Sabrina in that one bit is able to have the whole thing be pretty substantial. It’s not this fluffy bit. It’s a legitimate, well-constructed comedy.

That really broke something open for me back then. Especially because that was around the time that I started working at “S.N.L.” on the writing side, and I wasn’t really bringing anything about Asianness into my writing just because there was no delivery system for it. Then once I got on the cast, I was energized and invigorated by that Sabrina bit. Like, Oh, you can be the person delivering the jokes and also wink at how silly these characters are, but also wield them and disarm the person who held them in the first place.

Atsuko Okatsuka on Tig Notaro

‘She plays at the height of people’s intelligence’

OKATSUKA I was a young comedian, and my stand-up comedy teacher at the time posted this clip of Tig, 13 years ago. She is a friend now, but before that she was a comedy idol of mine. She also directed my first HBO special, “The Intruder,” so it comes full circle with her. She’s unapologetically herself — as a comedian and person and artist — super deadpan, and her joke “No Moleste” plays on linguistics and uses that as the tension.

A lot of Americans only know one language, English, and so the idea of hearing the word moleste is like, Oh, it sounds like something that would have a negative connotation.

She does the classic ‘If-this-then-what’ setup. In stand-up comedy, that structure only works when you continue building out the bigger picture and go further, and she does that really well.

I think about this joke a lot because it’s so simple. It’s not overexplaining a situation. It’s trusting that all humans communicate similarly, and that we all have the ability to see the absurd in situations. That’s what I love about it: She plays at the height of people’s intelligence.

I challenge myself every day, when I write jokes for the next show, to do that. To trust the audience to get the absurdity in the really-difficult-to-talk-about things. Tig has talked about having cancer and was able to talk about it in a way that was funny. And I’ve talked about my mom with schizophrenia and having been undocumented and caretaking for my grandma. These things should be a drama, right? But to be able to laugh about it — like giving your grandma a bath — there is comedy in it.

Hari Kondabolu on Margaret Cho

‘There’s a before Margaret and after Margaret’

KONDABOLU To see an Asian woman on stage already was kind of shocking, not only an Asian woman, but an Asian woman that talked as freely as Margaret did. She was talking about race, she was talking about sexuality, she would go anywhere. So not only do you have the groundbreaking aspect of that visually, but you also have somebody who’s talking about territory I’ve never seen discussed before. So you know, as a 15-year-old, this opens up possibilities that didn't exist until I saw her.

She had the show, but she wasn't talking about it with gratitude. She was talking about it with a sense of, I deserve this. It was kind of shocking and amazing. It’s subtle, but the idea of “This is mine, this is what I have built” — that confidence affected me.

The fact that she was going after the name, because whenever our communities, especially then, were discussed, there had to be some kind of ethnic jab that was connected to some kind of racial joke. And so for her to immediately go after the names they were suggesting was like, That’s it, that’s exactly the experience! She’s knocking it right back down and showing how absurd it actually is.

This joke doesn’t necessarily seem new, but she was doing her own mother’s voice; she wasn’t a white person doing it. Growing up, all the other South Asian kids I would hang out with made fun of our parents’ accents — not around white people but amongst ourselves.

With Margaret, it’s tricky, right? You have to see it in the context of the early ’90s because now it can read as a caricature, but you have to remember what it was like when none of us existed publicly. It feels different when you’re the first to do it.

The door opens with Margaret. There’s a before Margaret and after Margaret, and I think that’s true with a lot of us. I didn’t think it was possible for me to do stand-up comedy until I saw this. This is the reason I do stand-up comedy, because Margaret Cho exists. That’s what opened up the world to me.

Hasan Minhaj on Russell Peters

‘His fingerprints are on all of us’

MINHAJ Russell Peters’s 2004 “Comedy Now!” special is one of the most seminal pieces of work for Asian American comedy and in the modern stand-up comedy landscape.

This thing gets uploaded online during the birth of YouTube, and for the first time the true brown perspective was broadcast to the world beyond the walled gardens of CBC, Comedy Central or HBO.

I’m a freshman in college and I’m back home over the holidays. And I’m watching this YouTube clip and my entire family is in the living room. I’m watching this bit with my parents, and they’re howling. Crying laughing.

What’s really interesting is Russell was a seasoned veteran of comedy at that point. And he really did grow up in a true multiracial society. His ability to dance in between all of those cultures flawlessly through his act outs and through his voices gave him this stamp of authority.

Every voice that he’s doing is so spot on, so specific. People know you grew up around these people. You know exactly what you're talking about.

What he was able to do by talking about racial diversity, he opened the door for me to open up the aperture further and talk about financial diversity. And so what’s so cool is, once again, he opened the faucet to all of these rich veins that had never been exposed in mainstream popular culture. It created his career. But if you look at a list of comedians after that moment in the fall of 2005, for the past 20 years, his fingerprints are on all of us.

The punchline to be a man is the thing that came out of nowhere. A lesser comic would do a joke or a tag that’s kind of predictable. You see it coming. The reason why it became so iconic is you don’t. But it’s a testament to the character work.

I think you could pretty much ask the majority of East Asian or South Asian comedians, if you asked to open before him he would let you open for him. So I got to open for him in Sacramento, my hometown. And it was one of the biggest thrills of my life. He made sure there were two tickets for my parents. He made sure they were able to come backstage. He gave them a hug. He really had such a deep reverence and love for us as a community. And it shaped who I am still to this day.
